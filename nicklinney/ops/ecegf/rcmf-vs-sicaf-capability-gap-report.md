## Purpose

This report presents a concise capability gap analysis between SICAF strategic intent and current RCMF operating reality. It is intended to identify where operational execution, control design, reporting, and accountability do not yet fully support the strategic governance model.

## Inputs

The assessment is based on:

- SICAF strategic intent as the target-state reference
- RCMF current-state practices as the operational baseline
- Available evidence from policy, process, reporting, tooling, and stakeholder accountabilities
- Management judgment on control criticality, implementation feasibility, and sequencing value

## Method

The comparison uses seven capability domains. For each domain, the assessment reviews target intent, current-state execution, observable evidence quality, and the resulting gap type. Gaps are rated for severity using their effect on decision quality, control reliability, operating consistency, and executive visibility.

## Analysis Structure

| Rating | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Low | Minor misalignment with limited impact on control or outcomes |
| Moderate | Material inconsistency that impairs reliability or management visibility |
| High | Significant deficiency affecting governance, control execution, or decision confidence |

## Executive Summary

The overall pattern indicates that SICAF expresses a stronger strategic governance model than the RCMF currently operationalizes. The largest gaps are not in intent, but in translation: accountability, evidence discipline, management reporting, and repeatable control execution require tighter operational design and governance routines. NickLinney.Ops can close the most consequential gaps by establishing clearer decision rights, normalizing control evidence, and implementing a disciplined management cadence that converts strategy into monitored practice.

## Domain Assessment

| Domain | SICAF strategic intent | RCMF operational reality | Gap severity | Key implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Governance and decision rights | Clear ownership, escalation, and approval pathways | Accountability exists informally, with variable decision logging and escalation discipline | High | Decisions may be delayed, inconsistent, or hard to defend |
| Control architecture | Controls are defined as a coherent governance system | Controls exist in practice but are not consistently standardized or mapped to intended outcomes | High | Control coverage and assurance confidence are uneven |
| Process execution | Core workflows should be repeatable and dependable | Execution depends on operator experience and local workarounds in several areas | Moderate | Consistency and resilience are vulnerable to personnel variance |
| Data and reporting | Leadership should receive timely, decision-grade information | Reporting is serviceable but not fully normalized, traceable, or trend-oriented | High | Executives lack a consistently reliable view of performance and risk |
| Tooling and automation | Strategic intent assumes scalable enablement and traceability | Tool support is partial and does not yet fully enforce process or evidence capture | Moderate | Manual effort remains high and traceability remains fragile |
| Roles and capability | Required roles and competencies should be explicit | Critical responsibilities are understood, but role boundaries and backup coverage need formalization | Moderate | Delivery risk rises when key individuals are unavailable |
| Assurance and improvement | Ongoing review and corrective action should be systematic | Review activity occurs, but challenge, issue closure, and lessons learned are not uniformly governed | High | Weak feedback loops slow improvement and leave recurring issues open |

## Capability Gap Analysis

| Gap area | Gap type | Current condition | Target condition | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decision governance | Ownership gap | Decisions rely too heavily on implicit authority and informal escalation | Named decision rights with required approval and escalation thresholds | Immediate |
| Control mapping | Design gap | Controls are not consistently mapped to strategic outcomes and evidence expectations | Unified control inventory with clear purpose, owner, evidence, and review cycle | Immediate |
| Evidence discipline | Data gap | Proof of execution varies by process and operator | Standard evidence model with retained artifacts and reviewability | Immediate |
| Reporting model | Data gap | Reporting provides snapshots more than governed performance insight | Standard KPI and KRI reporting with trend, exceptions, and management actions | Near term |
| Workflow standardization | Execution gap | Comparable activities may be performed differently across situations | Repeatable procedures with defined entry, decision, and closure points | Near term |
| Role clarity | Ownership gap | Operational knowledge is concentrated and backup responsibilities are incomplete | Formal RACI and cross-coverage for critical functions | Near term |
| Continuous assurance | Assurance gap | Issues are reviewed, but closure discipline is not fully systematized | Scheduled reviews, issue register, closure tests, and management attestation | Immediate |

## Prioritization Logic

Priorities were assigned using the following criteria:

- Impact on executive decision quality
- Effect on control reliability and evidence defensibility
- Dependency value for later improvements
- Exposure created by inconsistent execution
- Relative effort to reach a governed baseline

## Recommended Decisions

1. Establish a formal governance baseline for decision rights, control ownership, and review approvals.
2. Create a single evidence and reporting model for core RCMF activities.
3. Standardize the most critical operating procedures before expanding automation.
4. Introduce a recurring assurance cadence with issue tracking, closure testing, and executive review.

## Conclusion

The gap between SICAF and RCMF is manageable but material. The minimum viable path forward is to strengthen governance mechanics, normalize evidence and reporting, and make accountability explicit enough that operational execution can be reviewed, measured, and improved without depending on informal knowledge alone.
